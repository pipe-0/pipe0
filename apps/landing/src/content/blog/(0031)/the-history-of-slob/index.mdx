In 1976, you could rent a telephone shaped like a mouse. It had white gloves and yellow shoes, and Mickey Mouse held the receiver above
his head like a trophy. It cost $39.95 per month and was, by any standard, a
ridiculous object.

Henry Dreyfuss Associates, the design firm that gave the world the famous
Model 500 desk set, drew it. The phone was sold alongside Snoopy, Winnie the
Pooh, and a fake candlestick.

Why would a company with a legal monopoly, a twenty-year engineering standard,
and no competitors start selling phones shaped like cartoon mice?

Because it could already smell what was coming.

## Slob

We have a word for cheap, abundant, low-effort output made possible by a new
machine. In 2025, _slob_ was named Word of the Year by both
Merriam-Webster and the American Dialect Society.

The word is older than the machines. In Middle English, _slop_ meant a mud hole.
Later, it meant watery, low-quality feed for animals.

To call something _slop_ is to say it was not made for you. It was made _at you_,
in volume.

Humans have been accusing themselves of producing _slob_ for about five hundred
and fifty years.

We are good at it.

### Venice, 1481

The first great slop panic did not concern the printing press. It concerned
the people operating it.

Hieronimo Squarciafico worked as an editor for Aldus Manutius, the finest
printer in Europe.

In 1481, he wrote that printing had fallen into the hands of unlettered men.

Within three years, a dozen book presses started operations, flooding Venice's
book market with low-quality editions of previously hand-copied Greek and
Latin classics.

The market had collapsed.

A Venetian monk named Filippo de Strata famously said, "The pen is a virgin.
The printing press is a whore."

In 1492, the Benedictine abbot Johannes Trithemius composed _De laude
scriptorum_ (In Praise of Scribes), a passionate defense of hand-copying
against the vulgar new machine. He argued that a monk copying scripture drew
closer to God, and that parchment (a writing material made from animal skin)
would outlast paper.

Ironically, he published his defense as a machine-printed book in 1494.

### London, 1852

A civilisation organised around its disgust.

The Great Exhibition of 1851 flooded Britain with machine-made ornament,
mass-produced wallpaper, and papier-mâché trays. Henry Cole mounted a
counterattack.

At Marlborough House, he assembled an exhibition called _Examples of False
Principles in Decoration_: eighty-seven everyday objects, each displayed with a
label explaining its crime. "Direct Imitation of Nature." "General want of
repose."

The press named it the Chamber of Horrors.

It was the first museum of slop. And it failed instructively.

The public did not recoil from the objects. They recoiled from Cole. Charles
Dickens's magazine published a satire of the display in December 1852. The
condemned wallpapers continued to be manufactured for decades.

The gatekeeper had assumed that people bought ugly things because they had
not yet been told the rules. He had it backwards. People were buying, for the
first time in the history of their class, things at all.

### Rochester, 1888

Then the machine reached the hand.

George Eastman's Kodak camera cost twenty-five dollars and came with the film
already inside. You pressed the button; the company did the rest.

Within a decade, a million and a half roll-film cameras arrived in amateur
hands. New verbs entered the language: _kodaking_, _kodakers_. The newspapers
settled on a name for the people wielding them, and it was not a compliment:
_camera fiends_.

Early Kodak prints came back circular. A round crop hides the corners, where
amateurs ruin photographs.

The most successful consumer technology of the nineteenth century shipped
with a built-in anti-slob mechanism for concealing its users' incompetence.

### Everywhere, 1906

In 1906, John Philip Sousa published "The Menace of Mechanical Music."

Recorded music, he warned, would end amateur playing. "What of the national
throat? Will it not weaken?" he asked.

The reply from Columbia Phonograph's vice-president was harsh. If mediocre
pianists vanished, he said, we would be spared the horrors of scales and
exercises in every house and flat in the country.

Sousa's essay was not only about aesthetics. It was part of a coordinated
campaign for what became the Copyright Act of 1909.

Before 1909, US copyright protected copies, and a record was legally not a
copy. A record company could buy a single set of sheet music for a Sousa
march, press thousands of discs from it, and owe him nothing.

The taste argument and the rights argument arrived in the same envelope, and
the taste argument was the one printed on the outside.

The complaint about the new machine's worthless, mass-produced output
threatened the people being paid by the old one.

### London, 23 May 1982

Barry Manilow went on tour with synthesisers instead of a string section. The
string players noticed.

On 23 May 1982, the Central London branch of the Musicians' Union passed a
motion calling for an outright ban on synthesisers and drum machines.

In a different part of the world, teenagers were buying discarded drum
machines and inventing hip-hop and techno "slob" on them.

### America, 1984

Which brings us back to the telephone.

For most of the twentieth century, Americans did not own their telephones.
They rented them.

The phone in your hallway belonged to a company called AT&T. It had been
built by Western Electric, which AT&T owned, and leased to you by a local
Bell company, which AT&T also owned.

Phone sets had the renting arrangement moulded into the plastic housing:
"BELL SYSTEM PROPERTY. NOT FOR SALE".

The famous Model 500 desk telephone was built to last twenty years. It was,
by any standard, a well-crafted phone and a good business. Every failure
meant a van, a technician, and a wage; all on AT&T's side of the ledger.

In 1968, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) voted six to nothing
that AT&T could not forbid you from attaching your own equipment to the
network.

Within a few years, the American market was full of cheap, low-quality
telephones selling as low as $3.95. Instead of beige boxes, these phones came
in strange colors and shapes. AT&T called such devices _foreign attachments_,
and it argued that strangers' equipment would damage the network; a safety
case, made on behalf of the public.

In 2000, a Hong Kong company called VTech paid $113.3 million for what was
left of AT&T's consumer telephone business, and acquired the exclusive right
to put the letters "AT&T" on a telephone. The AT&T phone you can buy today is
made by the descendants of the slop.

### Cupertino, 1985

The Macintosh shipped in 1984 with something no "cheap" machine had carried
before: a screen made of pixels rather than characters, and more than one
typeface.

The disaster required one more year and two more products. In 1985, Apple
shipped the LaserWriter and Aldus shipped PageMaker.

Five hundred years of typographic craft collided with several million people
who had just discovered that fonts were free.

The output acquired a name that survives as a technical term: the _ransom note
effect_. Untrained users mixed all sorts of things, just because they could.

To lean in on the "slob" joke, early Macintosh system software included a
bitmapped typeface by Susan Kare called San Francisco, whose entire design
was the ransom note effect. Later, Microsoft commissioned a font called
Ransom in 1992. The mockery became a product.

Beneath the mockery, an extinction. The International Typographical Union,
one of the oldest craft unions in America, lost half its membership between
1984 and 1987. Over a hundred and fifty thousand typesetting machines had
been sold in the preceding decades. Between 1985 and 1995, the typesetting
trade essentially ceased to exist.

How did the ransom notes stop?

Not through regulation, and not through shame. Through a paperback. In 1990,
a writer named Robin Williams published _The Mac is Not a Typewriter_, a short
book explaining smart quotes, kerning, leading, widows, and orphans. In 1994,
she followed it with _The Non-Designer's Design Book_, which taught several
million people the rules of good typography.

That is what the end of a slop era looks like. Not a ban. A grammar, cheap
enough to buy at an airport.

## The law

Six centuries, six technologies, one law.

First, a tool escapes the guild that owned it. Not a better tool, usually a
worse one, judged by the guild's own standards.

Second, the volume explodes and the median output is genuinely
bad. This is not a misperception by snobs. The $4 telephone really did break.
The ransom-note newsletter really was unreadable. The critics are describing
something true.

Third, a grammar arrives, from a rulebook, from a market that stops rewarding
failure, or simply from a generation that grew up with the tool and never had
to unlearn anything. And then the tool goes invisible, which is the highest
status any technology can achieve.

Watch the vocabulary across the whole sequence: _unlettered men_. _False
principles_. _Camera fiends_. _The national throat_. _Loonies_. _Ransom note_.
_Pigswill_. The complaint is rarely about quality. It is about who is allowed
to make things, dressed in the language of quality.

Ultimately, the tools matured. The crafts did not. The scribes did not come
back. The typesetters did not come back. The string players did not come
back. And the twenty-year telephone never came back either.

The pattern is simple: the technology survives its slop era, and the guild
does not. Both halves are true, and only one of them is comforting.

_Written in 2026, with the help of a machine that is currently producing
several million pages an hour, most of them bad._
