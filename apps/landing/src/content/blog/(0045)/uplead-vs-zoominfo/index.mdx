## Same category, opposite selling motion

- Both are B2B contact databases, so unlike Clay vs ZoomInfo this is a
  legitimate like-for-like (link the clay-vs-zoominfo post for the
  category map).
- The split is the buying motion. UpLead: self-serve signup, published
  prices, month-to-month available, 7-day trial with 5 credits.
  ZoomInfo: sales-led, quote-only, annual contracts; its pricing page
  is not even fetchable without a session. One of these vendors lets
  you know the cost before a call; that difference is worth naming as
  a real feature.

## The real prices

- UpLead, from the live pricing page: Essentials $99/mo for 170
  credits, Plus $199/mo for 400 credits, ~25% off annual ($74/$149),
  Professional custom and annual-only. Extra credits $0.60 each on
  Essentials, ~$0.50 on Plus. One credit unlocks one contact or
  company; re-downloading an unlocked record is free.
- The ToS details that function as price increases, quoted so readers
  do not learn them at renewal: credits do not roll over ("use it or
  lose it" is the contract's own language), all fees are
  non-refundable, annual plans need 60 days' cancellation notice.
  Reviewers on three platforms independently report auto-renewal
  charges without notification, against the help center's claim of a
  clean process; flag the gap and link both.
- ZoomInfo, from buyer-reported data since no list price exists:
  median contract $33,500/yr, observed range $7,200 to $155,000+,
  entry commonly ~$15,000, auto-renewals carrying 5-10% escalators
  plus credit-overage fees (Vendr data via hackingdemand.com; note
  the provenance honestly, it is the best available, not official).
  ZoomInfo's own UpLead comparison page now claims "free to start,
  consumption-based", which contradicts every third-party contract
  floor; likely a new self-serve tier [verify what that tier actually
  includes before publishing].
- The per-record floor comparison that matters at SMB scale: UpLead
  Essentials works out to ~$0.58 per unlocked contact ($99/170).
  Against a $15,000 entry contract, that is the whole argument for
  self-serve at low volume; the argument flips only when you consume
  ZoomInfo's gated extras (org charts, intent, Chorus).

## The 95% guarantee, decoded

- The finding, stated plainly: there is no guarantee document. The
  /guarantee and /data-accuracy-guarantee URLs 404; the phrase lives
  only in marketing pages. The Terms and Conditions say the opposite,
  in capitals: UpLead "MAKES NO EXPRESS, IMPLIED OR STATUTORY
  REPRESENTATIONS, WARRANTIES, OR GUARANTEES... RELATING TO THE...
  ACCURACY OR COMPLETENESS OF ANY INFORMATION." No refund clause for
  bad data exists in the contract.
- What the "guarantee" actually is: a billing mechanic. At the moment
  you unlock a contact, UpLead runs a live SMTP check. Invalid: you
  are never charged (there is no refund because there was no
  purchase). Valid or Accept-All: charged and delivered. It is a good
  mechanic, and materially more honest than batch-verified databases;
  it is just not a warranty, and the distinction matters.
- Scope limits readers should know: the check covers deliverability
  at export time only (B2B data decays an estimated 20-30% per year,
  so a list bought in January is not 95% anything by June); phone
  numbers are excluded entirely, with multiple independent reviewers
  converging on 55-65% phone accuracy [no first-party figure exists;
  mark as reviewer-reported]; strongest in North America.
- The one field test in circulation, with provenance caveat: a
  500-contact bounce test (cited via prospeo.io, original was a
  Reddit experiment) measured UpLead at 4.1% hard bounces, Apollo
  6.8%, ZoomInfo 9.2%. Directionally, the export-time check earns
  its keep; 4.1% bounce is roughly consistent with the 95% claim.

## Accuracy is published; coverage is not

- Teach the distinction in two sentences: accuracy is "of the records
  delivered, how many are right". Coverage is "of the people you
  actually looked for, how many exist in the database at all". A
  vendor can be 95% accurate and find only half your list; the axes
  are independent, and only one of them is marketed.
- The evidence that coverage is UpLead's real constraint, all from
  named reviews: the database leans on LinkedIn presence, so people
  without profiles are nearly unfindable; niche-vertical searches
  return "a lot less leads than expected"; "niche leads with mobile
  don't really exist"; limited direct dials versus ZoomInfo. No
  coverage percentage exists from any source, including UpLead.
- ZoomInfo's counterpart problem: its scale claims are unfalsifiable
  in the other direction. Its own comparison page says 500M contacts;
  Artisan says 260M; RB2B says 600M; Cognism says 174M emails. A 3.4x
  spread across sources describing the same database is not a rounding
  disagreement, it is the absence of a defined methodology. Say so.

## If you got here searching RainKing or DiscoverOrg

- The lineage, dates verified: RainKing and DiscoverOrg were both
  founded in 2007 and competed head-on in sales intelligence.
  DiscoverOrg acquired RainKing on August 28, 2017 (RainKing's 2016
  revenue: $33.9M). DiscoverOrg then bought Zoom Information in
  February 2019 and rebranded the combined company as ZoomInfo on
  September 10, 2019, seven months later. IPO June 2020 at $21,
  first trade opened at $40. The ticker changed from ZI to GTM in
  May 2025.
- The takeaway for the searcher: any article comparing RainKing to
  ZoomInfo is comparing a company to itself, five acquisitions late.
  Dataset lineage matters less than current refresh practice; ask
  when the record you care about was last verified, whoever you buy
  from.

## The decision

- Under a few thousand unlocks a month, US-centric, want to start
  today: UpLead. Watch the no-rollover clause, calendar the renewal
  notice, and treat phone numbers as unverified regardless of the
  email guarantee.
- Territory planning, org charts, intent, conversation intelligence,
  procurement in the loop: ZoomInfo, negotiated from the ~22%
  discount norm off first quote, with the escalator clause read
  before signing.
- Neither is wrong; they are priced for different volumes of the
  same job. The third option is not buying a database at all: pay
  per delivered record across many databases. That is the waterfall
  post's territory; end with the link, no summary.
